Acute Appendicitis: Causes, Risk Factors, and What to Know

Acute appendicitis occurs when the appendix becomes blocked, most commonly by:

Hardened stool (appendicolith) — the leading cause

Swollen lymphoid tissue following a recent infection

Foreign bodies (rare)

This blockage triggers bacterial overgrowth, inflammation, swelling, and potentially rupture if untreated.

Key risk factors include:

Age between 10 and 30

Male sex

Low-fiber diet

Family history of appendicitis

Smoking

Recent gastrointestinal infection

Symptoms, diagnosis, and treatment options vary, and urgent medical care is often required to prevent complications like rupture or peritonitis.

Explanation

What Is Appendicitis?

Appendicitis is inflammation of the appendix, a small, finger-shaped pouch attached to the large intestine. When the appendix becomes blocked, bacteria multiply inside, causing pain, swelling and, if left untreated, serious complications such as rupture. Understanding what causes appendicitis can help you recognize symptoms early and seek prompt medical care.

Key Causes of Appendicitis

Luminal Obstruction

Fecaliths (appendicoliths): Hardened stool can get stuck in the appendix opening, blocking its lumen.

Lymphoid hyperplasia: Swelling of lymphatic tissue in the appendix wall—often triggered by viral or bacterial infections—can close the lumen.

Foreign bodies: Rarely, swallowed seeds, parasites or other objects lodge in the appendix.

Bacterial Overgrowth

After an obstruction, normal gut bacteria (e.g., Escherichia coli, Bacteroides species) proliferate in the trapped mucus.

The overgrowth triggers inflammation, increased pressure and reduced blood flow, which may lead to tissue death and perforation.

Infection-Related Triggers

Gastrointestinal infections: Viruses (adenovirus, measles) or bacteria (Yersinia enterocolitica) can inflame lymphoid tissue, increasing obstruction risk.

Systemic infections: Less commonly, infections elsewhere in the body can stimulate immune cells in the appendix.

Genetic and Environmental Factors

Family history: Having a first-degree relative with appendicitis modestly raises your risk.

Diet low in fiber: Diets high in refined carbohydrates and low in fiber may contribute to harder stools and fecalith formation.

Smoking: Some studies suggest smoking increases appendicitis risk, possibly by affecting immune response or gut flora.

How Obstruction Leads to Inflammation

According to Bhangu et al. (2015), acute appendicitis follows a sequence:

Obstruction of the lumen reduces drainage.

Bacterial proliferation within the trapped contents.

Inflammatory response causes swelling of the appendix wall.

Increased pressure impairs blood flow (ischemia), leading to tissue death and potential perforation.

Without timely treatment, a blocked appendix can burst, releasing infectious material into the abdominal cavity and causing peritonitis—a life-threatening condition.

Who's Most at Risk?

Appendicitis can affect anyone, but certain factors make it more likely:

Age: Most common between ages 10 and 30.

Sex: Slightly more common in males.

Signs and Symptoms to Watch For

Early recognition of appendicitis can prevent complications. Common warning signs include:

Dull, crampy pain near the belly button, which shifts to sharp pain in the lower right abdomen

Loss of appetite, nausea or vomiting

Low-grade fever (often 99–100.5°F/37.2–38.1°C)

Abdominal swelling or bloating

Inability to pass gas

Preventing Appendicitis

While you can't eliminate all risk factors, certain lifestyle choices may help:

Eat a high-fiber diet: Fruits, vegetables, whole grains and legumes promote regular bowel movements.

Stay hydrated: Adequate fluid intake softens stool.

Practice good hygiene: Handwashing can reduce the chance of infections that stimulate lymphoid tissue.

Diagnosis and Treatment Overview

Doctors typically use a combination of history, physical exam, blood tests and imaging (ultrasound or CT scan) to confirm appendicitis. Treatment options include:

Surgery (appendectomy): The standard of care—can be open or laparoscopic.

Antibiotics: In select uncomplicated cases, antibiotics alone may resolve inflammation.

Early treatment reduces the risk of rupture, abscess formation and prolonged hospital stay.

When to Seek Medical Help

Appendicitis can progress rapidly. Contact a healthcare provider if you have:

Severe or worsening abdominal pain, especially in the lower right quadrant

Persistent vomiting or inability to eat/drink

High fever (over 101°F/38.3°C)

Signs of dehydration or dizziness

If you suspect appendicitis, don't delay—prompt evaluation is crucial. Always speak to a doctor about symptoms that might be life-threatening or serious.

Bottom Line

Appendicitis results primarily from blockage of the appendix lumen—due to fecaliths, swollen lymphoid tissue or rare causes like foreign bodies—followed by bacterial overgrowth and inflammation. Risk factors include age, diet, genetics and certain infections. Recognizing symptoms early and seeking prompt medical care can prevent complications.
