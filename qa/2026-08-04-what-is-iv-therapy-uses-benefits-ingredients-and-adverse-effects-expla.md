Intravenous (IV) therapy is a method of delivering fluids directly into the bloodstream. These fluids can be infused with vitamins, nutrients, medications, or other treatments to address health issues. When receiving IV therapy, a needle attached to a small, long tube called a catheter is inserted into a blood vein. The tube connects to a bag of fluids that is hung above the patient’s head. Let’s take a look at how IV therapy works, its potential benefits, and much more.

The 2 Delivery Mechanism Types of IV Therapy

There are two types of methods to carry fluids from the bag into the bloodstream.

Drip infusion – Gravity helps carry the fluids and any ingredients injected into them through the tube and into the bloodstream.

Bolus (Push) infusion – Motorized pump maintains a constant flow of fluids to the bloodstream.

What are the 5 R’s of Fluid Therapy?

While researching IV therapy, you may have come across references to the “5 R’s” of infusion therapy. This refers to the step-by-step process used by healthcare professionals to assess a patient and determine the best approach to IV therapy treatment.

Resuscitation – involves determining whether a patient needs IV therapy, determining the type of fluids needed, and administering them.

Routine maintenance – outlines the proper steps to deliver IV fluids over a period of time.

Replacement and Redistribution – these steps guide healthcare experts through adjusting IV formulas as necessary to address health concerns.

Reassessment – occurs multiple times throughout the treatment process to ensure a patient’s needs are being addressed properly.

The administration of Intravenous therapy involves a systematic process to ensure safety and efficacy.

IV Fluids

The primary ingredient in IV therapy is IV fluids, also known as saline solutions. IV fluids do more than just deliver vitamins and medications. IV fluids help hydrate the body, which is essential to proper body function. Hydration has two components – fluids and electrolytes.

Fluids and Tonicity

The volume-behavior of cells in a solution is known as tonicity. Cells have two parts- intracellular, which is the inside of the cell, and extracellular, which is the outside of the cell. Fluid may transfer between these two parts in a process known as osmosis to reach the ideal tonicity, which is the isotonic state.

Cells being in an isotonic state means that they have an equal concentration of fluids both inside and outside the cell. IV fluids come in three types of solutions to help a patient achieve tonicity:

Hypotonic fluids – Hypotonic fluids help move fluids intracellularly, which rehydrates cells and may cause cell swelling. These solutions are administered to address diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) or hyperosmolar hyperglycemia.

Isotonic fluids – Isotonic fluids increase fluid volume in the body. These types of fluids are typically used to address situations like blood loss, dehydration, or are administered during surgery. [1]

Hypertonic fluids – Hypertonic fluids transfer fluid extracellularly. This causes cells to shrink. These fluids are only used in specific situations, most commonly in Intensive Care Units (ICU) to treat pulmonary edema, a condition that results from fluid overload. [2]

IV Therapies in Professional Sports

In professional sports, the use of IV therapies for rehydration and recovery has been a subject of scrutiny. It is prohibited for athletes to use IV infusions for rehydration and recovery without prior Therapeutic Use Exemption (TUE) approval.

Some athletes may seek IV therapy for performance enhancement, raising concerns about fairness and adherence to anti-doping regulations. The controversies surrounding IV use in sports highlight the delicate balance between medical treatment, performance enhancement, and ethical considerations within the athletic community.
