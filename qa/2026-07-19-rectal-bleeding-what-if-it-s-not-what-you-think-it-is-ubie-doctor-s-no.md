Explanation

Rectal Bleeding—What If It's Not What You Think It Is?

Noticing blood when you're wiping or in the toilet can be alarming. While many people assume rectal bleeding is just hemorrhoids, it can sometimes point to other, more serious conditions. Understanding the possible causes, knowing when to seek help, and taking appropriate steps can help you address the issue early—without unnecessary worry.

Common Causes of Rectal Bleeding

Rectal bleeding refers to any blood coming from the anus, whether it's bright red on toilet paper or darker in the stool. Causes range from mild to serious:

HemorrhoidsSwollen veins in the rectum or anus.• Itchy or painful bumps around the anus• Bright red blood on toilet paper or in the bowl

Anal FissuresSmall tears in the lining of the anus, often due to passing hard stool.• Sharp pain during bowel movements• Bright red bleeding

DiverticulosisPouches in the colon wall that can bleed if inflamed (diverticulitis).• Sudden, painless bleeding• Can be heavy and stop on its own

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)Ulcerative colitis or Crohn's disease causes chronic inflammation in the digestive tract.• Frequent diarrhea, sometimes with blood• Cramping, urgency, weight loss

Colorectal Polyps or CancerGrowths on the colon lining that may bleed.• Occult (hidden) bleeding detected by stool tests• Changes in bowel habits, weight loss, fatigue

Upper GI BleedingSometimes bleeding from the stomach or esophagus appears darker (coffee-ground) or as black, tarry stool (melena).• Ulcers, gastritis, varices• Reference: Oakland K & Jairath V. define acute lower GI bleeding; but remember that upper sources can mimic it.¹

When It's More Than Hemorrhoids: Red Flags

Even if you've had hemorrhoids before, new or severe symptoms warrant a closer look:

Heavy bleeding or passing large clots

Dizziness, lightheadedness, or fainting

Rapid heartbeat or low blood pressure

Severe abdominal or rectal pain

Persistent bleeding despite home care

Unexplained weight loss or fatigue

If you experience any of these, do not wait—seek medical evaluation immediately.

The Role of Liver Disease in Bleeding

Bleeding can sometimes stem from liver disease rather than the colon or rectum:

Compensated CirrhosisIn early cirrhosis, the liver still works but scar tissue builds up. Over time, this can cause portal hypertension (high pressure in the vein carrying blood from the intestines to the liver).²

Esophageal or Rectal VaricesEnlarged veins that develop from portal hypertension can rupture and bleed heavily.

Hyponatremia and Mortality RiskLow blood sodium in cirrhotic patients predicts worse outcomes and increases bleeding risk.³

If you have liver disease, regular monitoring and prompt treatment of portal hypertension are essential to prevent life-threatening bleeds.

When to Seek Urgent Care

Call emergency services or go to the nearest ER if you have:

Profuse bleeding (soaking more than one pad per hour)

Blood clots or maroon-colored stools

Signs of shock: cold, clammy skin; rapid breathing; confusion

Severe abdominal pain or tenderness

Vomiting blood or passing black, tarry stools

Delaying care in these situations can lead to serious complications.

Medical Evaluation and Testing

To pinpoint the source of rectal bleeding, doctors may recommend:

Anoscopy or ProctoscopyDirect look at the anal canal and lower rectum for hemorrhoids or fissures.

Flexible SigmoidoscopyExamines the lower colon for polyps, inflammation, or bleeding sites.

ColonoscopyFull evaluation of the colon; can detect polyps, tumors, diverticula, and IBD.

Take-Home Points

Rectal bleeding isn't always hemorrhoids. It could signal diverticular disease, IBD, polyps, cancer, or varices.

Note bleeding details: color, quantity, accompanying symptoms (pain, dizziness).

Seek urgent care for heavy bleeding, clots, faintness, or other red-flag symptoms.

Early evaluation—through anoscopy, colonoscopy, or imaging—improves outcomes.

If you have known liver disease, be extra vigilant about variceal bleeding.

If you experience any life-threatening or serious symptoms—heavy bleeding, fainting, chest pain, or severe abdominal pain—please speak to a doctor or go to the nearest emergency department right away. Always discuss rectal bleeding with your healthcare provider to rule out serious conditions and get the treatment you need.
