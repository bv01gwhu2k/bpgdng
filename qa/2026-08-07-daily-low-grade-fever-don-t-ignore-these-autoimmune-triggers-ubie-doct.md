Why You Have a Low-Grade Fever Everyday: The Science of Autoimmune Triggers

Experiencing a low-grade fever every day can be unsettling. A persistent body temperature between 99°F and 100.4°F often signals that your immune system is active. While occasional mild fever is a normal response to infection, a daily low-grade fever—technically called "fever of unknown origin" when it lasts weeks—warrants a closer look. In many cases, autoimmune triggers play a key role. This guide explores why you might have a low grade fever everyday, the science behind autoimmune conditions, and practical steps you can take.

Common symptoms may include mild fatigue, occasional chills, or slight sweating. Unlike high fevers, you usually feel well enough to carry on daily activities. However, persistent elevation suggests an underlying issue.

Autoimmune Triggers and Chronic Inflammation

Autoimmune conditions occur when the immune system mistakenly attacks healthy tissues. This ongoing immune activity releases inflammatory chemicals called cytokines, which can raise body temperature slightly but consistently.

How Autoimmunity Raises Your Temperature

Interleukins (e.g., IL-1, IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signal the brain's thermostat (hypothalamus) to boost temperature.

Continuous low-level tissue injury keeps the immune response turned on.

Sometimes harmless proteins resemble foreign invaders, tricking the immune system into an ongoing attack.

Common Autoimmune Conditions Linked to Low-Grade Fever Everyday

Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA)Chronic joint inflammation, morning stiffness, low-grade fevers.

Lupus (Systemic Lupus Erythematosus)Skin rashes, joint pain, fatigue, subtle fever spikes.

Hashimoto's ThyroiditisUnderactive thyroid can cause mild fever, weight gain, cold intolerance.

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (Crohn's, Ulcerative Colitis)Digestive symptoms plus low-grade fever.

Sjögren's SyndromeDry eyes/mouth, joint pain, possible recurring mild fevers.

Other Causes to Consider

While autoimmunity is a frequent culprit, don't overlook:

Chronic infections (e.g., tuberculosis, sinusitis)

Hidden abscesses (dental, abdominal)

Medications (drug-induced fevers)

Malignancies (lymphoma, leukemia)

Endocrine disorders (hyperthyroidism, adrenal insufficiency)

A thorough evaluation by a healthcare provider helps rule out these possibilities.
