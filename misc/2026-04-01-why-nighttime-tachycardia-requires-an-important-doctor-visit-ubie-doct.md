# Why Nighttime Tachycardia Requires an Important Doctor Visit | Ubie Doctor's Note

Why Nighttime Tachycardia Requires an Important Doctor Visit

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Nighttime tachycardia — a resting heart rate above 100 bpm accompanied by palpitations or chest discomfort during sleep — can be triggered by sleep aids, stress, anxiety, caffeine, or underlying heart rhythm disorders like atrial fibrillation or SVT. Left unaddressed, these episodes disrupt restorative sleep and may signal cardiovascular conditions that require prompt medical evaluation.

Common causes of a racing heart at night include:

Sleep apnea and disordered breathing

Anxiety, panic attacks, or elevated cortisol

Medications, alcohol, or caffeine before bed

Thyroid imbalances or dehydration

Arrhythmias such as AFib or supraventricular tachycardia

Explanation

Experiencing a racing heart at night can be alarming. While occasional increases in heart rate are normal—especially after exercise, stress, or caffeine—persistent nighttime tachycardia (a resting heart rate above 100 beats per minute) deserves careful attention. In some cases, over‐the‐counter or prescription sleep aids may trigger a rapid heart rate. Understanding why this happens and when to seek medical care can help you stay safe and sleep soundly.

How Sleep Aids Can Trigger Rapid Heart Rate

Many people turn to sleep aids for better rest. However, some of these medications and supplements can affect your heart rhythm:

Antihistamines (e.g., diphenhydramine)

Melatonin in high doses

Herbal remedies (e.g., valerian root, kava)

Prescription sedatives (e.g., zolpidem, benzodiazepines)

These substances may:

Interact with other medications

Increase adrenaline or cortisol levels

Alter electrolyte balance

Cause dehydration

When any of the above lead to a faster heartbeat, you might notice palpitations, pounding in the chest, or difficulty breathing—especially if you're lying still in bed.

Why Nighttime Episodes Are Concerning

Reduced DistractionAt night, your body is winding down. A sudden surge in heart rate feels more dramatic when you're lying awake in a quiet room.

Potential Underlying ConditionsNighttime tachycardia can unmask hidden issues such as supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), atrial fibrillation, or other rhythm disorders.

Sleep DisruptionRepeated awakenings due to rapid heartbeats interfere with restorative sleep, impacting mood, cognition, and overall health.

Ambiguous SymptomsChest discomfort, dizziness, or mild breathlessness may feel subtle in daylight but can become distressing at night.

Common Causes of Nighttime Tachycardia

While a sleep aid causing rapid heart rate is one possibility, other triggers include:

Stress and anxiety

Caffeine or nicotine close to bedtime

Alcohol or recreational drugs

When to Schedule a Doctor Visit

Seek medical attention if:

Episodes occur more than once a week

You wake up in the middle of the night with a pounding heart

You experience additional symptoms (dizziness, chest pain, fainting)

Over‐the‐counter remedies and lifestyle changes don't help

You have a history of cardiovascular disease, high blood pressure, or diabetes

Your doctor can determine whether your nighttime tachycardia is benign or a sign of something more serious.

Understanding Supraventricular Tachycardia (SVT)

One specific rhythm disorder that often appears at night is supraventricular tachycardia (SVT). SVT is an abnormally fast heartbeat originating above the heart's ventricles. It can cause sudden-onset palpitations, dizziness, and shortness of breath.

Tips for Better Sleep Without Risky Sleep Aids

Adopting healthy sleep habits can reduce your reliance on medications that may cause rapid heart rate:

Stick to a consistent sleep schedule

Create a dark, quiet, and cool bedroom environment

Wind down with calming activities (reading, gentle stretching)

Limit screen time at least an hour before bed

Avoid large meals and excessive fluids close to bedtime

Practice deep breathing or progressive muscle relaxation

When to Head to the Emergency Room

While most cases of nighttime tachycardia don't require emergency care, go to the ER if you experience:

Severe chest pain or pressure

Sudden fainting or collapse

Extreme shortness of breath

Signs of a stroke (weakness on one side, slurred speech)

These could signal a life‐threatening event.
