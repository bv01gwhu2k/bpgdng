Explanation

Your heart is at the center of your health. Whether you're feeling fine or experiencing unusual symptoms, knowing how doctors check on your heart—and how certain medications or supplements, like sleep aids, might affect it—can help you stay safe and informed.

Why Heart Monitoring Matters

The heart can change over time, sometimes without obvious symptoms.

Early detection of rhythm disturbances, structural issues or signs of strain can prevent serious problems.

Some common medications, including certain sleep aids, may influence heart rate, blood pressure or rhythm.

1. Medical History and Physical Exam

Your first step is usually a conversation with your doctor and a hands-on exam:

• Medical history – Past heart conditions (arrhythmias, high blood pressure, valve issues) – Family history of heart disease or sudden cardiac events – Lifestyle factors (smoking, exercise, stress, caffeine, alcohol, sleep habits) – Use of prescription or over-the-counter medications, including sleep aids

• Physical exam – Checking pulse rate and rhythm at the wrist or neck – Listening to heart sounds with a stethoscope (murmurs, extra beats) – Measuring blood pressure – Assessing fluid status (swelling in legs or around the lungs)

2. Electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG)

A basic ECG records the heart's electrical activity. It's quick, painless and can be done in a doctor's office or clinic.

What it shows:

Heart rate (beats per minute)

Heart rhythm (regular or irregular)

Signs of past or recent heart injury

Why it's useful:

Detects atrial fibrillation, ventricular ectopy, conduction delays

Helps determine if further monitoring or treatment is needed

3. Ambulatory Heart Monitors

If your ECG is normal but you still have symptoms—like palpitations, dizzy spells or chest discomfort—your doctor may recommend wearing a portable monitor.

Types of monitors:

Holter monitor: Records continuously for 24–48 hours

Event recorder: Activated by you when you feel symptoms (can record for weeks)

An echocardiogram uses ultrasound to create images of your heart in motion.

5. Stress Testing

Your doctor may want to see how your heart responds under exertion. There are two main types:

Exercise stress test: You walk or run on a treadmill while your ECG and blood pressure are monitored.

Pharmacologic stress test: For those who can't exercise, medications mimic exercise by increasing heart workload while under ECG monitoring.

Stress tests help uncover blockages in coronary arteries and assess overall cardiovascular fitness.

6. Advanced Imaging and Studies

In some cases, additional studies are needed:

CT Coronary Angiography: Non-invasive X-ray images of blood vessels.

Cardiac MRI: Detailed pictures of heart muscle and blood flow.

Electrophysiology Study (EPS): Invasive test mapping electrical pathways to locate sources of arrhythmia.

Can Sleep Aids Cause Heart Problems?

Many people rely on over-the-counter or prescription sleep aids to get a good night's rest. While they can be effective, some carry potential cardiovascular risks:

• Antihistamine-based sleep aids (e.g., diphenhydramine) – May cause a fast or irregular heartbeat in susceptible individuals – Can raise blood pressure in some people

• Benzodiazepines and "Z-drugs" (e.g., zolpidem) – Generally safer for the heart when used short-term under supervision – Higher doses or long-term use can contribute to low oxygen levels during sleep, stressing the heart

• Melatonin and herbal remedies (e.g., valerian, chamomile) – Lower risk of direct heart effects – Quality and dosage vary widely; talk to your doctor before starting

Risk factors that increase the chance of sleep aid–related heart issues:

Existing heart disease or rhythm disturbances

High blood pressure

Sleep apnea or other breathing disorders

Concurrent use of other medications affecting the heart

What Symptoms to Watch For

If you're taking sleep aids—or any medication—and notice new or worsening heart-related symptoms, discuss them with your doctor:

Palpitations (fast, fluttering or pounding heartbeat)

Dizziness, lightheadedness or fainting

Shortness of breath, especially when lying flat

Chest discomfort or pressure

Unexplained fatigue or weakness

Lifestyle Tips to Protect Your Heart

Beyond regular checkups, simple lifestyle changes can reduce your risk of heart problems:

• Improve sleep hygiene – Keep a consistent bedtime and wake time – Create a dark, cool, quiet bedroom environment – Limit screens and bright lights at least 1 hour before bed

• Choose sleep aids wisely – Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration – Avoid mixing with alcohol or other sedatives – Discuss interactions with your doctor or pharmacist

• Manage cardiovascular risk factors – Stay active: aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate exercise each week – Eat a balanced diet rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains and lean proteins – Maintain a healthy weight – Control blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar
