The earliest and most obvious sign of this year's flu is a sudden high fever. And the dominant strain is going to strike hard and fast, Davis warns. While cold and flu viruses have similar incubation periods (between 1 to 2 days), this year's flu induces more rapid symptoms compared to a cold, which usually starts gradually with a sore throat and congestion. Also, high fevers are rare for a cold.

“The high fever might make someone realize they are actually sick because it is what typically makes you feel so bad,” says Jarod Fox, MD, an infectious disease specialist at Orlando Health. Though he notes other flu symptoms—chills, headache, body aches, and sore throat—still apply to the new influenza strain, and likely to be more severe. Why Experts Are Bracing for a Rough Flu Season

There are two main types of Influenza A viruses that are actively monitored all over the world: H1N1 and H3N2. In the Southern Hemisphere, where they wrapped up their early winter season, reports showed H3N2 was the dominant flu virus.

“Historically, H3N2 carries risk for more severe illness and lower vaccine effectiveness leading to more challenging influenza seasons,” Davis says. To make matters worse, the Southern Hemisphere’s winter season ended with the H3N2 variant (subclade K) starting to evolve. Surveillance data from the United Kingdom confirmed it was this variant that has become the dominant strain for their ongoing flu season.

Why does this matter? The biggest issue is that scientists didn’t anticipate this when making this year’s flu vaccine. Davis explains that the H3N2 component of the current flu vaccine was designed to protect against the J.2 subclade, not the K strain of the virus.

“The new flu strain is just different enough from the current vaccine strain and the previous years' strains that it will be able to evade the immune system better,” says Fox. “Early reports suggest it may also be on the more severe side.”

"This mismatch between H3N2 vaccine strain and the circulating subclade K has many concerned that vaccine effectiveness may be even lower than hoped in what is typically a rough season when H3N2 dominates," Davis adds.
