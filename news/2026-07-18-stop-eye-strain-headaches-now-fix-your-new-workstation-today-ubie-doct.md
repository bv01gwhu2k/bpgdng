Preventing Eye Strain and Headaches at Your New Workstation

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Preventing eye strain and headaches at a new workstation requires a combination of ergonomic adjustments, lighting control, and healthy daily habits.

Key strategies to prevent workstation-related eye strain and headaches:

Optimize monitor settings: Adjust brightness to match your surroundings, increase contrast for easier reading, and use a refresh rate of at least 60Hz (higher if available).

Position your screen correctly: Keep the top of the monitor at or slightly below eye level, about 20–28 inches from your eyes.

Control ambient lighting: Reduce glare by positioning your screen perpendicular to windows and using soft, indirect lighting.

Practice good posture: Sit with your back supported, feet flat on the floor, and shoulders relaxed.

Take movement breaks: Follow the 20-20-20 rule—every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds.

Stay hydrated and manage stress: Dehydration and tension are common but overlooked headache triggers.

If eye strain, headaches, or related symptoms persist despite these adjustments, they could signal an underlying issue such as uncorrected vision problems, migraines, or another condition that needs attention. Why New Monitors Trigger Headaches and Eye Strain

When you upgrade to a monitor with higher resolution, different size, or unfamiliar settings, your visual system and posture often need a period of adjustment. Common culprits include:

Brightness and contrast mismatch: Excessive brightness or extreme contrast forces your eyes to constantly adjust.

Screen flicker: Even imperceptible flicker (from certain backlight technologies) can lead to visual fatigue.

Poor resolution or refresh rate: Low refresh rates and pixel density can cause noticeable judder and sharpness issues.

Improper viewing angle or distance: Sitting too close, too far, or off-center makes your neck and eyes strain to focus.

Inadequate ambient lighting: Harsh overhead lights or glare on the screen create competing light sources.

1. Optimize Monitor Settings

Adjust brightness to match your room lighting. A quick test: gazing at a white page on screen should feel neither glaring nor dim.

Set contrast so text is sharp but doesn't burn your retinas—too high, and edges flicker; too low, and reading becomes a squint-fest.

Enable any "flicker-free" mode your monitor offers. Many modern screens include DC backlighting that minimizes refresh-rate flicker.

Pick a refresh rate of at least 60 Hz; 75–144 Hz is even better for smoother motion and less visual fatigue.

Use "blue light filter" or "night mode" in the evening to reduce high-energy wavelengths that can interfere with sleep patterns.

2. Position Your Screen Correctly

Place the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level. Your gaze should be about 10–20 degrees downward.

Keep the monitor about an arm's length (50–70 cm) away. Adjust if you wear reading glasses or bifocals.

Center the monitor directly in front of you to avoid twisting your neck or torso.

3. Control Ambient Lighting

Eliminate direct glare by positioning the monitor perpendicular to windows or bright lights.

Use adjustable blinds or curtains to soften outdoor light.

4. Follow the 20-20-20 Rule

Eye muscles tire when held in a fixed position. Every 20 minutes:

Look at an object 20 feet (6 meters) away.

Hold your gaze for at least 20 seconds.

This simple habit relaxes your focus muscles and reduces cumulative strain.

When to Seek Professional Help

Most cases of headaches after new computer monitor use resolve with simple adjustments. However, persistent or worsening symptoms deserve attention. If you're experiencing recurring headaches, vision problems, or dizziness that doesn't improve with workstation adjustments, it may be helpful to check your symptoms to better understand what might be causing your discomfort and whether you should seek medical care.

Speak to a doctor if you experience any of the following:

Severe, sudden headaches unlike your usual pattern

Visual disturbances such as double vision, bursts of light, or persistent blurriness

Nausea, vomiting, or confusion accompanying the headache

Loss of coordination, weakness, or difficulty speaking

Any signs of increased pressure in the head (e.g., pounding pulse in the temples)

These could indicate more serious conditions requiring prompt medical evaluation.
